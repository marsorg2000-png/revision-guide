Theme Breakdown

The 7 Driving Factors of Medical Progress Across Four Eras

⚔️ War

Medieval

  • Lots of wars – practice for surgeons.
  • Hugh of Lucca in crusades – wine as antiseptic.
  • John of Arderne - the first English surgeon - pain killing ointment made from hemlock and opium.
  • Crusades – Islamic and Christian doctors put in touch eg Ibn-Sina.

Renaissance

  • French battle in 1537 Paré ran out of hot oil – used ointment instead.
  • Artificial limbs for wounded soldiers.

Industrial

  • Crimean War 1853 – shed a light on lack of healthcare (Florence Nightingale).
  • Army leaders found 40% of volunteers were physically unfit to be soldiers (mainly impacted modern).
  • Franco-Prussian war 1871 national rivalry Pasteur (a Frenchman) and Koch (a German) - rivalry helped to fuel their search for vaccines.

Modern

  • WW1/ WW2: Government incentivised to spend on drugs and techniques; battlefields provided patients to practice on.
  • Governments needed to provide for soldiers coming back.
  • Plastic surgery – Queen’s Hospital, Kent 1917 – 1000 beds for facial wounds (Harold Gillies).
  • American William Coolidge invented more reliable glass tubes for X-rays (1913); Polish Marie Curie developed mobile X-ray units (1914).
  • 1914, doctors found sodium citrate stopped blood clotting so it could be stored.
  • Flemming started to work on bacteria because of the number soldiers died from infected wounds.
  • Lloyd George promised ‘homes fit for heroes’ following First World War; first council houses built. Evacuation during WW2 highlighted poverty; post-WW2 building of new towns and tower blocks.

⛪ Religion

Medieval Christianity & Islam

  • Christianity: Saved much of the knowledge from the Roman Empire. Controlled teaching eg promoted Galen. Banned human dissection. Causes/treatments: If God controlled everything involved in disease, no incentive to look for cures; treatment mainly involved praying/repenting. Hospitals: religion incentivised caring for people (more like care homes where nuns/monks looked after the weak). By 1400 – nearly 500 hospitals, including Bedlam (1247) for psychiatric care.
  • Islam: Encouraged taking care of diet, exercise, and hygiene. First hospital founded in Baghdad in 805, and by 1100s every large town had one. Preserved knowledge: built on ideas of Greeks and Romans; books saved were translated from Arabic to Latin.

Renaissance

  • In the plague, people believed God was the cause/treatment.
  • Henry VIII shut down monasteries and therefore hospitals.
  • People believed the touch of the King could cure illness because monarchs were chosen by God.
  • Some religious opposition to anaesthetics.

Industrial & Modern

  • Gradual secularisation of medicine as scientific discoveries and Germ Theory replaced religious explanations for illness.

🏛️ Government

Medieval

  • Didn't want to become unpopular by increasing taxes for sanitation.
  • York and London banned people from dumping waste in the street.
  • Quarantine attempted during Black Death.

Renaissance

  • Henry VIII set up the College of Physicians in 1518 to license doctors and stop quackery.
  • Infected houses quarantined by guards/watchmen.

Industrial

  • 1802: Jenner given £10,000 for vaccination.
  • 1853: Compulsory smallpox vaccination.
  • 1848 Public Health Act (permissive; Chadwick report).
  • 1858 Great Stink: Bazalgette given £3m for London sewers.
  • 1867: Working-class men got vote. 1875 Public Health Act: Compulsory sewers, medical officers, clean water.

Modern

  • Liberal Government reforms (1906–1911): Free school meals, medical service, pensions, national insurance.
  • 1941 US government grants for penicillin mass production.
  • 1948 NHS established by Aneurin Bevan ("cradle to grave" care); TB and polio vaccinations state-funded.

🔬 Science & Technology

Medieval

Hippocrates taught clinical observation.

Renaissance

  • Vesalius human dissections (corrected 300+ errors).
  • Harvey human circulation (1628).
  • Jenner vaccination (1796). Printing press spread knowledge.

Industrial

  • John Snow cholera statistics.
  • Pasteur Germ Theory & Koch specific bacteria. Microscopes proved germs real.
  • Anesthetics & antiseptics reduced surgical death rates from 50% to 15%.

Modern

  • Keyhole surgery, radiotherapy, laser surgery, DNA structure (1953).
  • Big Pharma mass production. Coolidge X-ray tubes (1913); Florey & Chain penicillin.

📡 Communication

Medieval

Crusades put Christian and Muslim doctors in contact; Ibn-Sina's Canon of Medicine.

Renaissance

Gutenberg's Printing Press (1440) allowed rapid sharing of medical texts.

Industrial

Pasteur's work translated and brought to England by Lister; published in The Times.

Modern

Internet / NHS 111 advice. Beveridge Report (1942) bestseller selling over 100,000 copies in first month.

🎲 Chance

Medieval & Renaissance

Hugh of Lucca noticing wine cleaning wounds; Paré running out of hot oil and improvising with soothing egg-yolk ointment.

Industrial

Great Stink (1858) hot summer causing Thames stench leading to Bazalgette's sewer system; Simpson's chance chloroform discovery.

Modern

Fleming accidentally discovering penicillin mould on a spore-contaminated petri dish.

The Black Death (1347–1348)

🦠 Causes & Beliefs

  • Believed: Position of stars, bad air (miasma), wells poisoned by Jews, punishment from God.
  • Real: Bacteria from fleas feeding off rats' blood; fleas passed disease to humans. Food shortages caused malnourishment.
  • Spread quickly because: Quarantine not effective in villages; street cleaning poor; unhygienic society; animals dug up quickly buried bodies.

💊 Remedies & Prevention

  • Remedies: Prayer, unusual cures like mercury, moving away if hearing about the plague.
  • Prevention: King Edward ordered Lord Mayor to clean London filth; quarantine (ships isolated for 40 days); flagellation (whipping selves to seek divine forgiveness).

📈 Impact

  • Social: Entire villages wiped out.
  • Political: Peasants wanted more money; feudal system weakened due to malnourishment and worker shortages.
  • Religious: Priests fled or died.
  • Economic: Food shortages, soaring food prices, landowners switched to sheep farming, farm workers demanded higher wages.

The Renaissance & The Great Plague (1665)

🎨 Cultural Rebirth

Cultural movement began in Florence. Promoted education and researching things themselves (“rebirth of learning”). Thought education in art could promote a better life.

Consequences:

  • Exploration: More accurate maps.
  • Printing: Explore medical teachings.
  • Art: Body portrayed in realistic detail.
  • New Inventions: Gunpowder (different wounds).
  • Science: More scientific approach to learning.

💀 The Great Plague (1665)

  • Killed 100,000 in London (¼ city population / almost 69,000 recorded in London that year).
  • Treatments: Bleeding with leeches, smoking away "poisoned air", sniffing sponge soaked in vinegar, moving to countryside (Charles II and Court).
  • Learning since Black Death: Noticed connection between dirt and disease; more organised public health orders; effective quarantine (watchmen); bodies in mass graves; fires lit; gatherings banned; trade limited.

Public Health Across Four Eras

🏰 Medieval & Renaissance Public Health

Medieval Problems & Improvements:

  • Water from river/storage pits; cesspits near water supplies; rubbish thrown in streams/streets; cattle roaming.
  • Improvements: Night carts collected waste; rakers cleaned streets; Exeter aqueducts built; Newcastle paved streets; laws passed against human/butcher waste.
  • Monasteries: Wealthy water supplies/sanitation, isolated river locations, clean monks showing devotion.

Renaissance Hospitals:

  • 1720–50: 5 new in London; 20,000+ patients/yr by 1800; specific wards; free treatment; Edinburgh (1776) free pharmacies.

🏭 Industrial & Modern Public Health

Industrial Conditions & Acts:

  • Slum conditions in industrial cities. Chadwick Report (1842): disease caused by bad air/overcrowding; need clean water. Laissez-faire government reluctance.
  • 1848 Public Health Act (permissive; slow adoption). 1858 Great Stink led to Bazalgette's £3m sewer system (83m sewers, 420m gallons water).
  • 1875 Public Health Act: Compulsory sewers, medical officers, clean water, rubbish collection.

Modern Reforms:

  • Booth (30% London poor) & Rowntree (28% York poor) poverty reports. Boer War recruitment (40% unfit).
  • Liberal Reforms (1906–1911): Free school meals, school medical service, old age pensions, labour exchanges, national insurance.
  • Beveridge Report (1942) & NHS launched by Aneurin Bevan in 1948 ("cradle to grave" care).

Causes, Treatments & Preventions Across Eras

Medieval

Causes: God, astrology, miasma, Four Humours imbalance.

Treatments: Bleeding, herbal remedies (herbals), Zodiac man, prayer.

Prevention: Cleaning streets, prayer, 40-day ship quarantine, flagellation.

Renaissance

Causes: Miasma, humours, alchemy (chemical causes), astrology, scurvy (dietary).

Treatments: Bloodletting, herbal remedies, opium, lemons/limes for scurvy (Woodall 1617), King's touch, alcohol.

Prevention: Quarantine, plague doctors (herb masks), pest houses.

Industrial

Causes: Microbes (Germ Theory by Pasteur), spontaneous generation debated, Koch specific germs.

Treatments: Anesthetics (Laughing gas 1800, Ether 1842, Chloroform 1847), Antiseptics (Carbolic acid 1867), Aseptic surgery.

Prevention: Public Health Acts, Jenner vaccination (1796), Snow waterborne investigation.

Modern

Causes: Germ Theory accepted, DNA (1953), lifestyle/diet, genetics.

Treatments: Magic bullets (Salvarsan 606), Antibiotics (Penicillin discovered by Fleming, mass produced by Florey & Chain 1941), organ transplants, IVF, stem cells.

Prevention: Free vaccinations (1946–69), screening programmes, hygiene campaigns, 5-a-day.

Surgery Through Time

⚔️ Medieval & Renaissance Surgery

Medieval (c.1250–c.1500):

  • External injuries, amputations, cauterisation (hot iron), trephination (drilling skull).
  • Barber-surgeons pulled teeth; wound care used vinegar/wine; no anaesthetics, no antiseptics.

Renaissance (c.1500–c.1700):

  • Ambroise Paré introduced ligatures (silk threads to tie vessels, 1536) instead of hot oil/cauterisation.
  • Vesalius (1543) & Harvey (1628) improved anatomical knowledge. Still lacked anaesthetics and antiseptics.

💉 Industrial & Modern Surgery

Industrial (c.1700–c.1900):

  • Anaesthetics (Nitrous oxide 1800, Ether 1846, Chloroform 1847) enabled painless, longer operations.
  • Lister introduced Carbolic Acid antiseptic (1867); aseptic operating environments developed.

Modern (c.1900–Present):

  • Keyhole surgery, laser surgery, microsurgery, robotic surgery.
  • Transplants (kidney 1960, heart 1967 by Christiaan Barnard), open-heart surgery, radiotherapy, immunosuppressants (cyclosporine 1970).

Key Historical Figures Cards

Individual cards detailing discoverers, reactions, English impacts, general impacts, and success factors.

Galen

Medieval

Discoveries: Theory of Opposites expanding Four Humours; diet and exercise treatments; brain controls speech; arteries carry blood; animal dissections (pigs/apes), leading to anatomical errors.

Reaction: Church agreed; little alternative, so people accepted his work; wrote 350 books.

England Impact: Highly respected in medieval England; ideas lasted long due to tradition and Church backing.

General Impact: Hindered medical progress for centuries due to unchallenged anatomical errors.

Factors: Church support; communication (350 books, but no printing press limited immediate impact).

Hippocrates

Pre-medieval

Discoveries: Formulated the Four Humours theory (everything needs to be in balance).

Reaction: Many people used his work and built upon it (e.g., Galen).

England Impact: Guided healthcare practices and theories in England for centuries.

General Impact: Doctors still swear the Hippocratic Oath today.

Factors: Individual (Galen built upon work); Religion (Church support); Science & Tech (lack of alternative methods).

Andreas Vesalius

Renaissance

Discoveries: Published The Fabric of the Human Body; corrected 200+ Galen errors (human breastbone parts); highlighted human/animal differences.

Reaction: Some backlash for disproving Galen; appointed court physician to Emperor Charles V.

England Impact: Translated in England by Thomas Geminus as Compendiosa (3 editions 1545–1559), fueling anatomical understanding.

General Impact: Forced shift away from blind acceptance of Galen; laid foundations for Harvey.

Factors: Communication (printing press); Government (protection from Charles V).

Ambroise Paré

Renaissance

Discoveries: Replaced cauterization with soothing egg-yolk ointment; introduced ligatures for amputations (1536); designed prosthetics; published Les Oeuvres (1575).

Reaction: Traditional surgeons resisted; gained battlefield recognition; royal surgeon to King Henry II of France.

England Impact: Translated into English; English surgeons adopted ligatures and new techniques.

General Impact: Revolutionized battlefield surgery; elevated surgery from brutal trade to skilled profession.

Factors: Chance (ran out of hot oil); War (continuous testing grounds).

William Harvey

Renaissance

Discoveries: Proved blood pumped continuously by heart; veins flow in one direction; published On the Motion of the Heart and Blood (1628).

Reaction: Criticized for contradicting Galen; hampered by lack of microscope technology for capillaries.

England Impact: Served as physician to King James I and King Charles I.

General Impact: No immediate practical cures, but enabled future breakthroughs; key moment in Scientific Revolution.

Factors: Science & Tech (limited by microscope tech); Individual (rigorous observation).

Edward Jenner

Renaissance

Discoveries: Discovered cowpox prevents smallpox; published An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae (1798), coining "vaccination."

Reaction: Some thought it interfered with God; initially called a "quack" lacking full explanation.

England Impact: Popularized after royal family members vaccinated; Parliament awarded £10,000 (1802) and made vaccination compulsory (1853).

General Impact: Helped eradicate smallpox globally; replaced dangerous inoculation.

Factors: Science & Tech (inoculation basis); Government (financial grants and compulsory legislation).

John Hunter

Renaissance

Discoveries: Linked/differentiated gonorrhea and syphilis; taught mercury treatment; 14,000 specimen collection; published A Treatise on the Blood, Inflammation, and Gunshot Wounds.

Reaction: Highly respected; surgeon to King George III.

England Impact: Prominent surgeon at St. George’s; founded Hunterian Museum; trained Jenner; Surgeon General to army (1790).

General Impact: Advanced anatomical study and surgical training (note: mistakenly thought gonorrhea/syphilis same due to contaminated needle).

Factors: Communication (vast collection); Science & Tech (rigorous scientific methods).

Nicholas Culpeper

Renaissance

Discoveries: Translated Latin/Greek texts into English; published The English Physician (1652) on medicinal herbs; rejected expensive foreign medicines; emphasized astrology.

Reaction: Challenged by medical establishment for promoting self-treatment; seen as eccentric due to astrology.

England Impact: Empowered ordinary people to care for health using natural local remedies; opened door to alternative medicine.

General Impact: Pioneered vernacular medical literature.

Factors: Communication (writing in English); Culture (public desire for accessible remedies).

Thomas Sydenham

Renaissance

Discoveries: Emphasized clinical observation; argued diseases remain consistent ("English Hippocrates"); introduced laudanum for pain; published Medical Observations (1676).

Reaction: Met resistance from those attached to Four Humours.

England Impact: Advanced clinical practice and observation methods in English medicine.

General Impact: Shifted medicine toward empirical, scientific approach.

Factors: Science & Tech (focus on observation over ancient texts).

Louis Pasteur

Industrial

Discoveries: Developed Germ Theory; proved microorganisms spoil liquids via swan-neck flask experiments on wine.

Reaction: Heavy opposition (Charlton Bastian spontaneous generation); acceptance grew after solving cattle plague (1866).

England Impact: Joseph Lister translated and applied his work in England; endorsed by John Tyndall.

General Impact: Permanently changed diagnostic processes and medical treatment globally.

Factors: War (Franco-Prussian war competition with Koch).

Robert Koch

Industrial

Discoveries: Isolated specific bacteria for TB, cholera, and anthrax; developed "Koch’s postulates"; pioneered staining and growing bacteria.

Reaction: Initially skeptical, but accepted after anthrax identification (1876); miasma defenders.

England Impact: Translated into English, allowing British doctors to build on microbiological foundations.

General Impact: Provided definitive scientific proof for Germ Theory; revolutionized bacteriology.

Factors: Science & Tech (advanced stains/microscopes); War (Franco-Prussian rivalry).

Edwin Chadwick

Industrial

Discoveries: Authored 1842 Report on Sanitary Conditions; linked poverty to disease spread; proposed infrastructure reforms (drainage, clean water).

Reaction: Local councils and taxpayers resisted costs; seen as overreach.

England Impact: Sparked public health officers across Britain; led directly to Public Health Act of 1848.

General Impact: Triggered modern state-sponsored public health reforms.

Factors: Chance (1847 cholera outbreak); Communications (widely published reports); Government.

Florence Nightingale

Industrial

Discoveries: Proved soldiers died from hospital infections over battle injuries; pioneered medical statistics and polar area diagrams.

Reaction: National war hero; faced sexism minimizing her impact as "just a nurse."

England Impact: Established Nightingale Training School at St Thomas’ Hospital; transformed hospital hygiene.

General Impact: Modernized nursing into a respected, disciplined profession.

Factors: War; Communication (innovative visual diagrams).

John Snow

Industrial

Discoveries: Proved cholera waterborne link (1854 Broad Street pump); debunked miasma for cholera; promoted safe anesthetic doses.

Reaction: Initially dismissed as miasma dominated; lacked immediate microbiological proof.

England Impact: Revolutionized epidemiology through his famous "ghost map."

General Impact: Gave authorities concrete preventative measures for epidemics.

Factors: Communications (ghost map); Publications (On Ether); Science & Tech (hindered before Germ Theory).

Charles Booth

Modern

Discoveries: Researched and mapped poverty in East London (1886); demonstrated direct statistical connection between poverty and ill health.

Reaction: Some critics claimed poverty rates were exaggerated.

England Impact: Published Life and Labour of the People in London, showing 35% lived in poverty; pressured government toward social reform.

General Impact: Laid groundwork for welfare policies.

Factors: Communications (detailed mapping); Government (prompted Liberal Reforms).

Alexander Fleming

Modern

Discoveries: Discovered penicillin accidentally in 1920s while looking for Staph cure; identified antibacterial mold byproduct.

Reaction: Not accepted instantly because he could not isolate or mass-produce it.

England Impact: Sparked antibiotic revolution in Britain.

General Impact: Created world's first true antibiotic.

Factors: Chance (spore contamination); War (military medicine urgency later).

Howard Florey & Ernst Chain

Modern

Discoveries: Realized therapeutic value of Fleming’s penicillin; developed methods to purify and mass-produce it (1940s).

Reaction: Broadly celebrated for saving countless lives during wartime.

England Impact: Saved thousands of Allied soldiers; accelerated recovery times.

General Impact: Made antibiotics globally available to masses post-WWII.

Factors: Government (US government backing/funding); Individual (built on Fleming).

Seebohm Rowntree

Modern

Discoveries: Researched poverty in York (1899), finding 28% in poverty; applied scientific criteria for minimum living standards.

Reaction: Dismissed by some contemporaries claiming scale was overstated.

England Impact: Acted as societal wake-up call; proved structural link between poverty, malnutrition, and poor health.

General Impact: Encouraged early welfare state; published Poverty: A Study of Town Life.

Factors: Communications (influential sociological study).

William Beveridge

Modern

Discoveries: Identified five "Giant Evils" (Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, Idleness); authored 1942 Beveridge Report.

Reaction: Opposed by some fearing heavy taxation; massive public backing (600,000 copies sold).

England Impact: Laid ideological foundations for 1911 National Insurance and 1948 NHS.

General Impact: Transformed Britain into a welfare state model.

Factors: Government (official sponsorship); War (WWII hardship); Communications (bestseller).

Aneurin Bevan

Modern

Discoveries: Minister for Health (1945–1951); established NHS; advocated "cradle to grave" comprehensive healthcare.

Reaction: Criticized by opponents fearing high cost or "something for nothing."

England Impact: Dismantled financial barriers to healthcare across England, vastly improving public health standards.

General Impact: Created one of the world's largest publicly funded healthcare systems.

Factors: Government (direct political leadership, funding, parliamentary passage).

"How useful is Source A..."

  • Para 1 (Content): Describe/quote source + make inference + contextual knowledge.

📝 Q3: Similarities (8 Marks)

"Explain two ways in which X and Y were similar..."

  • PEE 1: State similarity + specific details from both time periods.
  • PEE 2: State second similarity + link to broader historical context (Never write about differences).

"Has X been the main factor in..."

  • Cover at least two time periods and two distinct factors (one must be stated factor).
  • Structure with clear judgement, PEEL paragraphs, and conclusion.

Full Historical Glossary & Key Terms

Complete definitions of every essential medical history term from your course.

Anesthetic

A drug given to patients to put them into a deep sleep so they feel no pain during surgery (e.g., ether, chloroform).

Antiseptic

Chemicals used to kill bacteria and prevent infection in wounds (e.g., Lister's carbolic acid).

Four Humours

Ancient Greek theory that health depended on the balance of four bodily fluids: blood, phlegm, black bile, and yellow bile.

Germ Theory

Louis Pasteur's discovery that microorganisms (germs) cause disease and decay.

Laissez-faire

'Let it be'—the 19th-century government belief that it was not their job to interfere in public health.

Miasma

The belief that foul smells and bad air from rotting matter caused disease.
